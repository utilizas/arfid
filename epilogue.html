Epilogue: seeing what the frame leaves out

TipIn plain words

Paying attention to body image has helped many young people. This book argues for widening the lens: some young people struggle with food for other reasons, and the adults and tools around them need to be able to see that too. Doing so does not require new alarm. It requires better questions, better measurement, and tools that know their limits.

What the frame leaves out

The Introduction began with a frame: the pathway from idealised bodies through comparison and dissatisfaction to disordered eating, which has organised research and public concern about young people, screens and food. Each chapter then examined something that frame leaves out of view.

1  Counting a moving target: context and prevalence showed that ARFID’s frequency cannot be read off a single number, because clinical caseloads, registers, population surveys and screening questionnaires count different things, and that the quality of the measurement changes the answer. It also showed, through the arithmetic of screening, why a tool used mainly by people without the disorder must route rather than label. 2  A family of difficulties: related disorders, ages and groups placed ARFID in the family of feeding and eating disorders, described its sensory, appetitive and fear-driven presentations as dimensions that combine, and located the groups in which risk concentrates. 3  Seed and soil: genetic and environmental determinants explained why a high heritability is compatible with powerful environmental effects, and why the environments that matter most for ARFID may be the family table, the body’s own lessons and the social world of a child, acting as triggers and maintaining loops rather than as causes of liability.

4  Digital soil: social platforms, recommender systems and ARFID then turned to the digital environment. The evidence linking social media to body-image concern and disordered eating is substantial; the evidence linking it to ARFID is almost non-existent, not because the link has been tested and found absent but because the question has rarely been asked. The chapter set out seven candidate pathways, from vicarious fear learning to the advice parents meet online, and graded most of them as hypotheses. 5  Scribe, not judge: generative AI for early recognition and referral asked whether generative AI could help adults notice difficulties earlier, and answered with a qualified yes: only if the model is confined to the role of scribe, if urgency is decided by fixed rules, and if the protocol is evaluated before anyone relies on it.

A frame is not an error. Every way of seeing selects, and the body-image frame selected something real and important. The problem arises when a frame becomes invisible: when measures, moderation policies, clinical habits and software are all built inside it, and the young person who restricts food for other reasons falls outside every one of them at once.

Recommendations

For research

  1. Test the hypotheses, starting with the most tractable. The pathways graded H in Table 4.3 are testable. Audits of recommendation chains seeded with interests relevant to ARFID, and experiments on vicarious fear learning with food stimuli in adults, are feasible now and should be pre-registered.
  2. Measure content, not hours. Existing ARFID cohorts, including the genetic and registry studies described in From twins to genomes, and gastroenterology cohorts, could add brief, content-specific measures of media use. Data access for vetted researchers under the Digital Services Act (European Commission, 2025a) offers a route to the platforms’ own data.
  3. Evaluate the tools before deploying them. The vignette protocol of Evaluation protocol, with its ARFID-specific outcome of frame fidelity, is offered as a template. Its results should be published with their data and repeated whenever the models tested change.
  4. Close the language gap. Spanish-language data on ARFID, and evaluations of AI tools in languages other than English, are scarce. Both are needed if recognition is not to depend on the language in which a family asks for help.

For practice

  1. Look where risk is concentrated. In a meta-analysis, 16.27% of people with ARFID had an autism diagnosis (Sader et al., 2025), and ARFID symptoms are frequent among people with gastrointestinal conditions (Atkins et al., 2026; Flack et al., 2026). Autism services, gastroenterology and anxiety services are natural places to ask about restricted eating.
  2. Teach that “not about weight” does not mean “not serious”. Professionals, teachers and parents should be able to recognise restriction driven by sensory difficulty, lack of appetite or fear, and to refer it with the same seriousness as restriction driven by weight concern.
  3. Ask what, not how long. In clinical and school conversations, questions about the kind of content a young person meets online (food fears, elimination diets, “safe-food” communities) are more informative than questions about screen time, and questions about where parents’ advice comes from are more useful still.

For governance

  1. Prefer duties of design to duties of category. Content rules written for body image will miss content relevant to ARFID, which is mostly lawful and harmless item by item. Obligations that act on recommender systems themselves (limiting reliance on implicit engagement signals, interrupting repetition, offering feeds not based on profiling) reach it, as the European guidelines on minors already begin to recognise (European Commission, 2025b).
  2. Include ARFID-relevant risks in systemic-risk assessments. Platforms’ assessments of risks to minors’ physical and mental well-being under the Digital Services Act (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2022) should consider fear-related and diet-related content as well as appearance-related content.
  3. Hold general-purpose assistants to public, repeated tests. Assistants that answer questions about eating from young people and their parents should meet the safeguards set out in Design principles, and should be audited regularly against open vignette sets, with results reported by model and version.

Tools between worry and care

Most of the people this book hopes to reach are worried rather than certain. A parent notices that the list of foods a child will eat keeps getting shorter; a teacher sees a pupil avoid the lunch hall; a young person realises that fear of choking has quietly reorganised their day. Worry of this kind is a form of attention, and it deserves to be taken seriously. It is also fallible, and a tool that sits between worry and care can fail in two opposite directions. It can amplify worry into alarm, labelling as a disorder what will pass with time; or it can dissolve worry into reassurance, telling a family that a child is “just picky” when the child needs help. The arithmetic of 1  Counting a moving target: context and prevalence shows that the first failure is the more common one for any tool used widely; the evidence of 4  Digital soil: social platforms, recommender systems and ARFID and 5  Scribe, not judge: generative AI for early recognition and referral shows that the second is the more likely when the tool’s frame does not include the young person’s difficulty at all.

The protocol in this book tries to steer between those failures by being modest about what it knows. It does not decide what anyone has. It helps people say clearly what they have seen and take it to someone who can assess it, and it says plainly when something cannot wait. That modesty is not only a regulatory convenience. It is an epistemic stance: a tool should claim no more certainty than its evidence supports, and should make its own frame visible, including the dimensions it asks about, the rules it applies and the things it cannot see.

The same standard applies to this book. Its grades, dated searches and inspectable code are there so that readers can check its claims and find its mistakes. Some of its hypotheses will be wrong; the evidence-gap map is meant to be redrawn. If the book succeeds, it will be because the young people who restrict food for reasons other than their bodies become a little easier to see, for the adults around them and for the tools those adults are beginning to use.
