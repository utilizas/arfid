Interactive toolkit

TipIn plain words

Two tools live on this page. The first shows why quick questionnaires flag many people who turn out not to have a disorder. The second helps you write a clear prompt for an AI assistant that will organise what you have noticed and prepare you for a conversation with a professional. Neither tool gives a diagnosis, and nothing you enter leaves your browser.

Why screening tools over-flag: a base-rate simulator

Every screening question set has a sensitivity (how many true cases it catches) and a specificity (how many non-cases it correctly clears). When a condition is uncommon, even a good screen produces more false alarms than true cases. Move the sliders to see how the positive predictive value (PPV), the share of positive screens that are real cases, changes with prevalence. The presets use the pooled estimates in Nicholls-Clow et al. (2024) and the upper bound for eating-disorder services in Sanchez-Cerezo et al. (2023). Default sensitivity and specificity are illustrative, not those of any specific instrument.

Note composer for early recognition and referral

This composer builds a prompt you can paste into any AI assistant. The routing box is calculated by fixed rules on this page, not by the AI (see Design principles). The prompt asks the assistant to work as a scribe, not a judge: it organises your observations and prepares you for an appointment, and it is instructed never to diagnose, never to give numbers about food or weight, and to tell you when help is urgent.

WarningBefore you start

Do not type names, dates of birth or other details that could identify anyone. This page stores nothing, but the assistant you paste the prompt into may keep conversation records.

What have you noticed about eating?
How is it affecting daily life and health?
Anything else a professional should know?
Is any of this happening now?

Nothing is sent anywhere by this page. The red-flag list is a short lay summary, not a clinical checklist; professionals rely on fuller guidance such as MEED and NICE NG69.

How the composer maps onto the protocol

  • Patterns correspond to the three ARFID presentations plus a body-image item that switches the referral framing to a broad eating-disorder assessment (Calibrated prompt sequences).
  • Impact items mirror the consequences named in the diagnostic criteria (nutritional, medical and psychosocial), expressed without numbers.
  • Routing levels: urgent (any red flag), soon (growth, weight or supplement dependence), routine (other observations). The rules are deliberately simple and visible; they are a safety floor, not a triage instrument.
  • The canonical prompt texts will be published as an appendix once the vignette evaluation (Evaluation protocol) is complete; until then, the text this page generates is the working version.
