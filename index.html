What this book is

This book does three things at once. It is an academic essay on the environmental conditions in which feeding and eating disorders arise in adolescence. It is a practical guide for health and education professionals. And it offers an interactive toolkit: a set of calibrated prompt sequences for large language models (LLMs) that help adults organise what they have noticed and take it to the right professional.

Its focus is avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID). ARFID was added to the diagnostic manuals in 2013 (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), and it does not fit the story that dominates public debate on young people, social media and eating. That story is about body image. ARFID, by definition, is not.

Why “beyond body image”

Most research and policy on social media and eating disorders follows a single pathway: exposure to appearance ideals, social comparison, body dissatisfaction, then restriction (Dane & Bhatia, 2023; Holland & Tiggemann, 2016). The pathway is real and well documented. It is also partial. Children and adolescents can restrict food for reasons that have nothing to do with their bodies: food can feel or taste intolerable, eating can hold no interest, or a single frightening episode of choking or vomiting can make eating feel unsafe (Bryant-Waugh et al., 2022; Zickgraf & Ellis, 2018).

Instruments built to detect weight and shape concerns are, in this respect, like smoke detectors in a house with a gas leak: sensitive, reliable and silent about the hazard that matters. In a validation study with Turkish adolescents, ARFID subscales were largely unrelated to weight- and shape-related eating attitudes (Çelik et al., 2026). This book asks what follows, scientifically and practically, when we take that silence seriously.

Three ways to read it

Conventions

  • Each chapter opens with an In plain words box: a short summary in accessible language.
  • Claims about the evidence carry a grade where the evidence is thin or contested: E1 systematic or meta-analytic evidence; E2 several consistent primary studies; E3 single studies, case reports or cross-sectional associations; H hypothesis with mechanistic plausibility only.
  • Clinical terms are given in British English, with Spanish equivalents in the Glossary.
  • Every figure states its source in author–date form. Simulations are labelled as such; their code can be unfolded beneath the text, and the numbers quoted from them are computed when the book is rendered.
  • Searches and web sources are dated. Helplines on the If you are worried page were checked on the date shown there.
  • Following safe-communication practice, the book gives no figures for weight, calories or portions, and no images of bodies.

What this book is not

It is not a diagnostic instrument and does not replace assessment by a qualified professional. The toolkit does not score, label or store anything. Software that is intended to support diagnostic decisions falls under medical-device regulation in the European Union (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2017); this book deliberately stays on the other side of that line.

How this book was made

The book was written with the assistance of a large language model, used for literature retrieval, drafting, data analysis and code, and for the bilingual terminology work shared with the translat-ia workshop. Every source was checked against its primary record, bibliographic metadata for indexed articles were generated programmatically from PubMed, and the author takes full responsibility for the content. The same principle the book applies to its toolkit (the model as scribe, not judge) applied to its writing.

I am grateful to Carmen Moreno, a student on the Doctoral Programme in Languages, Texts and Contexts at the University of Granada, for her dedication to developing professional competence of the highest level in specialised translation. The need for expert professionals is evident in most of the content on health, asylum and the courts that people from different cultures and with different levels of literacy consult online every day. Errors and biases in information of this kind have potentially serious and often irreversible consequences, so human oversight of such content and of its multilingual dissemination should be among the binding criteria of corporate responsibility for public and private institutions alike. I hope that the efforts of the thousands of undergraduate and postgraduate translation students who view the arrival of advanced AI tools and systems with misgiving and uncertainty will prove worthwhile. Such systems may emulate certain aspects of expert human competence, but they can hardly ensure, let alone guarantee, the quality of their output without human supervision and verification.
