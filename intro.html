Introduction

TipIn plain words

Public worry about young people, screens and eating has focused almost entirely on body image. That worry is justified, but it leaves out young people who restrict food for other reasons, such as fear, sensory discomfort or lack of appetite. This book looks at those young people, at the environments around them, and at whether AI tools can help adults notice problems earlier without causing new harm.

A blind spot in a crowded field

A frame decides what is in the picture. It also decides, silently, what is not. Public and scientific discussion of young people, social media and eating has been organised by a powerful frame for two decades: exposure to idealised bodies leads to comparison, comparison to dissatisfaction with one’s own body, and dissatisfaction to dieting and disordered eating (Dane & Bhatia, 2023; Holland & Tiggemann, 2016). The frame is not wrong. Meta-analyses find that online appearance comparison is substantially associated with body-image concern and eating-disorder symptoms (Bonfanti et al., 2025), experiments show that idealised and “fitspiration” images increase body dissatisfaction in the short term (Fioravanti et al., 2022), and audits and internal company research show that recommender systems deliver more eating-disorder-related content to young users who signal vulnerability (Center for Countering Digital Hate, 2022; Horwitz, 2025). 4  Digital soil: social platforms, recommender systems and ARFID reviews this evidence in detail.

What the frame leaves out is restriction that has nothing to do with the body. Since 2013, the diagnostic manuals have recognised avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID): eating so restricted in range or amount that it causes weight loss or faltering growth, nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements or marked interference with daily life, without the concern about weight or shape that defines anorexia nervosa (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Three presentations are usually described: avoidance based on the sensory characteristics of food, lack of interest in eating, and fear of aversive consequences such as choking or vomiting (Zickgraf & Ellis, 2018). They frequently combine (Three dials rather than three boxes).

ARFID is not rare, although how common it is depends heavily on how one counts. A pre-registered meta-analysis of 26 studies found that weighting studies by their methodological quality lowered the pooled prevalence estimate from 11.14% to 4.51% (Nicholls-Clow et al., 2024), and 1  Counting a moving target: context and prevalence shows why estimates from clinical caseloads, registers, population surveys and screening questionnaires differ by two orders of magnitude. It is strongly heritable, with a twin-based heritability of 0.79 (Dinkler et al., 2023), which, as 3  Seed and soil: genetic and environmental determinants explains, says nothing against the importance of environments and nothing against treatment. And it does not always respect the frame’s boundaries: a nine-year-old with ARFID and severe malnutrition could value thinness, absorbed from social media, while restricting for entirely different reasons (Barney et al., 2022).

The thesis of this book follows. Environments shape feeding and eating disorders through more than one pathway. Attending only to the body-image pathway produces a systematic blind spot, in research, in clinical recognition and in regulation. Any AI-assisted tool built on that frame will inherit the blind spot and may automate it.

Why now

Three developments make the argument timely. The first is the growth of ARFID research itself. PubMed records naming ARFID in their title or abstract numbered close to a thousand by November 2026 (How little light: a bibliometric check), and large genetic and registry cohorts are under way (Bulik et al., 2023; Hog et al., 2025). Yet almost none of this work examines digital environments: of 14 records combining ARFID with social-media terms, none reported an ARFID-specific analysis of digital exposure (Table 4.2).

The second is that generative AI is already where worried adults and young people take their questions. One psychiatrist has argued that therapy-like conversation has become the single most frequent reason people use AI (Frances, 2026). Rule-based chatbots have shown modest benefits for eating-disorder prevention in a randomised trial (Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2022), but general-purpose chatbots have repeatedly produced restrictive diets, concealment advice and other harmful content when tested with eating-disorder prompts (Center for Countering Digital Hate, 2023, 2025), and generative dietary advice in adolescent and eating-disorder scenarios has mixed sound guidance with restrictive recommendations (Tumanggor et al., 2026). None of these evaluations concerns ARFID.

The third is a regulatory moment. The European Union’s AI Act, amended in July 2026 by the Digital Omnibus on AI, sets out when AI systems used in health count as high-risk and when those obligations apply (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024, 2026). Guidelines under the Digital Services Act now ask platforms to design recommender systems with minors’ safety in mind (European Commission, 2025), and Australia has begun enforcing a minimum age for social media accounts, with early evaluation data already published (eSafety Commissioner, 2026). Decisions are being taken now about which content categories count as harmful and which tools count as medical. If ARFID is absent from the evidence on which those decisions rest, it will be absent from the decisions.

Argument and structure

The book has two parts. Part I, The landscape, establishes what is known about ARFID. 1  Counting a moving target: context and prevalence asks how many people are affected and how we know, using the image of a coastline whose length depends on the ruler used to measure it, and draws from the arithmetic of screening a principle that runs through the book: a tool used mainly by people without the disorder must route people to assessment rather than label them. 2  A family of difficulties: related disorders, ages and groups places ARFID within the family of feeding and eating disorders, treats its three presentations as dials rather than boxes, and identifies the groups in which risk is concentrated: autistic young people and those with ADHD, anxious young people, and those with gastrointestinal and other chronic conditions. 3  Seed and soil: genetic and environmental determinants takes up genes and environments through the image of seed and soil, explains what a high heritability does and does not imply, and proposes a working model in which environments act less on liability than as triggers and maintaining loops.

Part II, Digital environments and generative AI, turns from what is known to what is not. 4  Digital soil: social platforms, recommender systems and ARFID reviews the evidence on social media and eating, shows by a reproducible bibliometric check how little of it reaches ARFID, and proposes seven candidate digital pathways (from vicarious fear learning to the advice parents meet online), each graded and set out in an evidence-gap map (Table 4.3). Its policy conclusion is that, for ARFID, duties of design that act on recommender systems themselves reach further than rules about named categories of content. 5  Scribe, not judge: generative AI for early recognition and referral sets out a protocol in which a language model acts as a scribe rather than a judge: urgency is decided by fixed rules, observations are organised by dimension without scores, and a second model audits every draft. The chapter specifies an evaluation, with synthetic vignettes and pre-registered criteria, that should precede any claim that the protocol works. The interactive toolkit implements both the screening arithmetic of 1  Counting a moving target: context and prevalence and the prompt sequences of 5  Scribe, not judge: generative AI for early recognition and referral.

The Epilogue draws the argument together and makes recommendations for research, practice and governance. A bilingual page for anyone who is worried offers practical guidance and verified sources of help, and a bilingual Glossary, designed as a teaching resource for specialised translation, sets out the English and Spanish terminology.

Method and standards of evidence

This is a critical, evidence-graded synthesis, not a systematic review. Sources were identified through targeted, structured searches, mainly in PubMed, and through a curated corpus of studies and grey literature on social media, AI and eating disorders assembled for an earlier project. Bibliographic metadata for indexed articles were generated programmatically from PubMed records rather than typed by hand, and no reference entered the bibliography until its identifier had been checked. Reports by advocacy organisations, journalism and disclosed company documents are used where peer-reviewed evidence is absent, but they are identified as such and appraised in the text. Searches whose results are reported quantitatively are dated and their strings given (Table 1); the verification log, included in the archived release, records the rest.

Claims about the evidence carry a grade where it is thin or contested: E1 for systematic or meta-analytic evidence, E2 for several consistent primary studies, E3 for single studies, case reports or cross-sectional associations, and H for hypotheses with mechanistic plausibility only. The grades describe the support for a specific claim, not the quality of a source in general; a strong meta-analysis can support only a weak claim about ARFID. Every figure gives its source; simulations are labelled as such, their parameters are stated and their code can be inspected; numbers quoted from simulations are computed when the book is rendered.

The book follows safe-communication rules throughout: it gives no figures for weight, calories or portions, contains no images of bodies, and describes no behaviours in a way that could serve as instructions.

The limits of this method should be clear. Searches centred on PubMed will under-represent work indexed only in psychological, educational or computing databases and in Spanish-language bibliographic sources; the latter is a known gap that further work will address. The author writes from the philosophy of technology and the study of science, technology and society, not from clinical practice: clinical statements rest on the cited evidence, and the toolkit has not yet been evaluated with the people it is meant to serve. The book should be read as an argument that can be checked, and corrected, at each step.
